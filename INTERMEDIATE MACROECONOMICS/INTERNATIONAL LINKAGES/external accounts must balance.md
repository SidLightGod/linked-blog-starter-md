1. the central point of the international payment is very simple : the individuals have to pay for what they buy abroad. 
2. if a person spends more than his income his deficit needs to be financed by selling of assets or by borrowing.
3. similarly if a country spends more abroad than it earns from abroad then the deficit needs to be financed by selling of assets or by borrowing abroad. 
4. these sale of assets or borrowing implies a current account surplus. thus any current account deficit is of necessity financed by an offsetting capital inflow. 
5. $$ Current \space account \space deficit + net \space capital \space inflow = 0 $$
6. this therefore states that if a country has no assets to sell, if it has no foreign currency reserves to use up, and if nobody will leads to it, then the country has to achieve balance in its current account. 
7. it is often useful to split the capital account into two separate parts : 1 the transactions of the country's private sector and 2 official reserve transactions, which corresponds to central bank's activities. A current account deficit can be financed by private residents selling off assets abroad, or borrowing abroad. 
8. alternatively or as well, the current account deficit can be financed by government, which runs down its reserves of foreign exchange, selling foreign currency in foreign exchange market. 
9. conversely when there is a private sector surplus, the private sector may use the foreign exchange revenues it receives to pay off debt or buy assets abroad
10. $$ balance \space of \space payments \ surplus = increase \space in \space official \space exchange \space reserves = current \space account \space surplus + private \space net \space capital \space inflow $$
11. if both the current and the private capital account is in the deficit, then the overall balance of payments in deficit, that is, the central bank is losing reserves. 
12. when one account is in surplus, the other one is in deficit, the overall deficit is zero - neither in surplus nor in deficit.
