"Extraordinary men have a right to commit any crime and to transgress the law in any way, just because they are extraordinary."  
Raskolnikov sees himself as someone capable of transcending the moral and legal constraints that bind ordinary people. Throughout the story, there's a tension between his ideal of being extraordinary and the emotional, psychological consequences of his actions. His case demonstrates how abstraction fails to account for the complexity of human emotions and the moral weight of real-life choices. We cannot function as pure nihilists, such detachment consumes us and leaves us hollow.
Raskolnikov is described as a skeptic, young, poor, and therefore cruel. He hates his poverty and feels alienated from society. Detached, isolated, and unsentimental, he views people like the pawnbroker as parasites. Killing her, he believes, would be justified if it meant helping many others, this utilitarian logic becomes the abstraction he clings to in order to justify his crime. But in doing so, he suppresses his own emotional turmoil. This is Dostoevsky’s core critique: abstraction and rationalization detach us from our inner world, from other people, and from the spiritual reality of our lives.
This critique is also central to _Notes from Underground_, where the narrator rejects systems that reduce human behavior to logic or reason. Like the Underground Man, Raskolnikov refuses to be “just another man.” His desire to be extraordinary isn’t merely intellectual, it’s existential. His abstraction from the ordinary becomes a way to avoid facing the real, human consequences of his actions. He idealizes a kind of life shaped by greatness and exception, as represented by figures like Napoleon, but in doing so, he blinds himself to the moral, emotional, and spiritual realities of being human.
Ultimately, Dostoevsky uses Raskolnikov to show how romanticized abstraction isolates individuals and leads to spiritual decay. His obsession with the "ideal" life reflects a broader philosophical problem: when we try to live by abstract theories alone, we risk severing ourselves from the very experiences love, guilt, suffering, redemption, that give life its meaning.
